How to choose your first AI agent workflow

2026-10-10 · AI agents, workflow, productivity

The most common mistake with AI agents is not a technical one. It is choosing the wrong task to automate first.

People reach for the biggest, most painful project in their week. That project is usually big because it is ambiguous, depends on other people, and changes shape halfway through. An agent will struggle, you will lose a week, and you will quietly decide agents are overhyped.

There is a better way to pick your first workflow. It comes down to four questions.

1. Does it happen at least once a week?

Frequency beats size. A task that takes twenty minutes but happens every day is a better first target than a task that takes four hours but happens twice a year.

Frequent tasks give you something rare and valuable: fast feedback. You will see within days whether the agent is doing well, and you will get many chances to correct it. A rare task gives you one shot and no learning.

2. Is the input already digital and already yours?

The best first workflows start from something you already have in a digital form — a document, a spreadsheet, an inbox, a folder of files, a URL.

If the first step of the task is "ask three people to send me things," that is a coordination problem, not an automation problem. Agents are excellent at handling information that already exists. They are not good at chasing humans.

3. Can you describe "done" in one sentence?

This is the single most useful test.

  • "Summarise every new support email and draft a reply in our tone." Done is clear.
  • "Improve our marketing." Done is not clear.

If you cannot say what finished looks like, the agent cannot either. It will produce something plausible and you will not be able to tell whether it is right. Start with tasks where you would know a good result instantly if you saw it.

4. Does a mistake cost you five minutes or five thousand dollars?

Start on the cheap side.

Early on, you are not just getting work done — you are learning how to brief, check, and correct an agent. That learning is much cheaper when the blast radius is small. Draft the newsletter, do not send it. Prepare the report, do not file it. Once you trust the pattern, raise the stakes deliberately.

A workflow that passes all four tests

Here is a shape that almost always works as a first project:

> Every morning, take everything that arrived overnight — emails, form submissions, comments, documents — summarise each item in two lines, sort them by urgency, and draft a reply for the ones that need one.

It is frequent. The input is already digital. "Done" is obvious. And a bad summary costs you almost nothing, because you read it before acting on it.

Notice what this workflow actually is: triage. Not creation, not decisions — sorting and preparing. Triage is where agents earn their keep first, because it is high volume, low ambiguity, and easy to verify.

What to avoid as a first project

Three patterns tend to fail early:

Work that depends on taste you have not written down. If your brand voice lives only in your head, the agent cannot match it yet. Write the rules down first — that exercise alone is usually worth the time.

Work that is mostly waiting. An agent that has to wait for a human approval in the middle of every run is not saving you much. Automate the part before and after the wait instead.

Work that is one long task with no checkpoints. A workflow with three verifiable steps is far more reliable than one giant instruction, because you can see exactly where it went wrong.

Start smaller than feels impressive

The goal of your first workflow is not to transform your business. It is to build one habit: delegating a real task, then checking the result.

That habit is what compounds. Once you trust the loop, the second workflow is easier to design, the third is obvious, and eventually you stop thinking of it as automation at all. It just becomes how the work gets done.

Pick something small, frequent and verifiable this week. Run it for seven days. Then expand.
