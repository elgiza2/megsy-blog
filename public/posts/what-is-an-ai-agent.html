What is an AI agent? A plain-English guide

2026-10-07 · AI agents, basics, productivity

Most people meet AI as a chat box. You type a question, you get an answer, you copy the answer somewhere else, and you do the actual work yourself. That is a tool. Useful, but it waits for you.

An AI agent is different in one specific way: it is built to finish the task, not to describe it.

The three things that make something an agent

1. It can take actions, not just produce text. An agent can browse a page, run a calculation, create a file, call another service, or hand work to a second agent. The output is a changed state, not a paragraph.

2. It keeps context over time. A chat session forgets. An agent remembers your project, your preferences, your constraints, and the decision you made last week. That memory is what turns ten separate requests into one piece of work.

3. It works in steps, and it can be interrupted. Real tasks have branches. An agent plans, executes, checks the result, and corrects. When something is ambiguous it asks, instead of inventing an answer.

A concrete example

Imagine you want a summary of your competitors' pricing pages.

With a chat tool you paste in text and ask for a summary. You find the pages, you copy them, you paste them, you re-paste them when the model loses track.

With an agent you say: check these six pricing pages, extract the entry price and what is included, put it in a table, and tell me which one is cheapest for a team of five. The agent opens the pages, reads them, builds the table, and answers. You review a result instead of assembling one.

The difference is not intelligence. It is autonomy over steps.

What agents are genuinely bad at

Honesty matters here, because the marketing around agents is louder than the reality.

  • Agents fail quietly. A chatbot that does not know something says so. An agent can confidently produce a finished-looking result that is wrong. This is the single biggest risk, and it is why evaluation and visible reasoning matter more than raw capability.
  • Agents need clear boundaries. Given a vague goal and open access, an agent will do something — just not always the thing you wanted. Scope is a feature.
  • Agents are not employees. They do not have judgement about your business, your customers, or your risk tolerance. You still own the decisions.

Where agents actually earn their place

The pattern that works today is narrow and unglamorous: repetitive, multi-step, verifiable work.

  • Collecting and structuring information from many sources
  • Producing first drafts that a human edits
  • Running the same check every day and reporting only what changed
  • Moving data between systems that do not talk to each other

Notice what these have in common: they are boring, they happen often, and you can tell whether the result is right. That last part is what makes them safe to automate.

The question to ask before adopting one

Not "is this agent smart?" but "if it gets this wrong, will I notice?"

If the answer is yes, you have a good candidate. If the answer is no, you have a future incident.

Where this is heading

The interesting shift is not one brilliant agent. It is several narrow agents, each with one job and one owner, coordinated by something that keeps track of the whole. Specialists are easier to trust, easier to evaluate, and much easier to replace when one of them starts drifting.
